Work out your odds before you bid

In an oversubscribed category, allotment is a computerised random draw. Nobody can tell you whether you will get shares. What can be worked out is the probability, the capital it blocks, and whether an extra lot buys you anything at all. That is what this page does. The desk splits in two: the mechanics and the evidence, and the calculator that runs on them.

How allotment actually works

The mechanics below are verified against real Basis-of-Allotment documents, not inferred from commentary.

Retail

Undersubscribed (≤1x): everyone gets what they applied for, in full.

More applications than maximum allottees: a computerised lottery, and every winner receives a maximum of one lot.

Fewer applications than allottees but oversubscribed on shares: everyone gets at least one lot, surplus pro-rata.

sNII and bNII

sNII: applications of ₹2,00,000 to ₹10,00,000, drawing on one-third of the NII reservation.

bNII: applications above ₹10,00,000, drawing on two-thirds.

The category is set by the value of your application, not by lot count, and the boundary shifts with every issue. Each winner receives the minimum NII bid, then any balance pro-rata.

QIB, anchor, employee

QIB: proportionate when oversubscribed, with mutual funds entitled to up to 5% of the QIB portion.

Anchor: up to 60% of the QIB portion, allocated at the issuer's discretion.

Employee: pro-rata, capped at ₹2,00,000 per employee, rising to ₹5,00,000 where the quota is undersubscribed.

The one-lot cap bites immediately above 1x Alpine Texworld was subscribed just 1.36 times in retail and allotted exactly one lot, 142 shares, to every successful bidder, whether they had applied for one lot or thirteen. There is no gentle middle band; the transition from full allotment to flat lottery is sharp. Caliber Mining is the cleanest proof: its published ratio of 62:1949 was identical for every bid size from one lot to forty-seven. Once retail is oversubscribed, extra lots on the same PAN buy nothing at all, the capital is blocked for zero additional chance.
Be careful with bNII when it is oversubscribed An oversubscribed bNII applicant blocks more than ₹10 lakh and receives roughly ₹2 lakh worth. Lohia Corp's bNII bidders applied for 2,380 shares and were allotted 490, the sNII minimum. Only when a category is barely oversubscribed does allotment approach the full bid: Manipal's bNII at 1.07x allotted 1,606 shares of a 1,700-share bid.

Where the calibration factor comes from

The textbook formula divides maximum allottees by the number of applications. Neither input is known before bidding closes, so it cannot be used to make a decision. What the data shows

Odds are far better on the mainboard

—

The reason is structural rather than incidental. Mainboard lots cost around ₹15,000, so a large retail base can afford to participate and the reservation is spread across many more allottees. SME minimum tickets run to ₹1–2.6 lakh, which concentrates the applicant pool but also means almost everyone bids the minimum, so subscription in shares and competition per applicant become the same thing.

Heavily subscribed issues listed better

—

This is a correlation on a small sample and it runs in the direction you would expect: demand that shows up in the subscription book also shows up on listing day. It is not a trading rule. The issues that were barely subscribed produced the flattest listings, and the one deeply negative outcome in the set came from an issue that was well subscribed.

The practical consequence The IPOs that are easiest to get an allotment in are, on this evidence, the ones that tended to list flattest, and the ones that listed best were the ones where the odds were worst. That tension is the whole problem. It is also why spreading applications across separate PANs matters more than sizing any single one: you cannot improve your odds on a hot issue by bidding more, only by applying more times, legitimately, through genuinely separate applicants.

Rules of thumb this supports

  • Apply in retail unless another category is visibly less subscribed on the day you decide.
  • Prefer larger issues. Odds improve sharply with issue size, because the reservation grows faster than the applicant pool.
  • Bid the minimum lot unless retail is tracking at or below roughly 1x late in the bidding window.
  • Spread across accounts rather than stacking lots. The same money, several times the chance.
  • Check subscription on day two or three. Day-one figures consistently understate final demand and will mislead you.
  • Treat bNII with caution when oversubscribed. It blocks over ₹10 lakh to deliver about ₹2 lakh.

What this does not do

  • It does not predict whether you will be allotted. In an oversubscribed category that is a computerised random draw and no model can tell you the outcome.
  • Odds are calibrated estimates from subscription multiples, not the exact ratios in each issue's Basis of Allotment.
  • The calibration rests on ten IPOs, nine of them mainboard and one SME. The SME factor is a single observation.
  • Spillover between categories is ignored. It materially affected at least one issue in the calibration set, where an undersubscribed sNII category released 876,114 shares into retail and made the realised odds better than the raw subscription figure implied.
  • Nothing here is investment advice, and none of it accounts for whether the company is worth owning after listing.
